## Reading · 9 min

**Full article first (4 min):** [Read the entire VOA article](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/grammar-and-halloween-/7326228.html) from the opening to the final cultural question and sign-off. Build a **whole-text model**, not a word-by-word translation:

1. **Opening:** Halloween date and language-learning purpose.
2. **Practice:** costumes, door-to-door requests, candy, frightening displays.
3. **Origins with uncertainty:** possible Celtic background of trick-or-treating.
4. **Vocabulary and adaptation:** scary / afraid / spooky; decorations; turnips becoming pumpkin lanterns in America.
5. **Application / conclusion:** an everyday dialogue and invitation to compare celebrations across cultures.

**Two selected passages from that same article (3 min):**

- **Selection A · origin and qualification:** Under **“Trick-or-treat,”** find the paragraph starting **“Some people believe the custom…”**. **Why selected:** it connects a modern action to a *tentative* historical explanation. **Sentence surgery:** subject = *Some people*; reporting verb = *believe*; content = *the custom goes back to…*. What does *the custom* refer to? Does *believe* mean *historians have proved*? Find the relative clause introduced by **in which** and say what it modifies.
- **Selection B · cultural adaptation and causality:** Under **“Halloween decorations,”** find the paragraph starting **“The custom of carving scary faces…”**. **Why selected:** it links the historical story to a concrete change of material. **Sentence surgery:** *is believed to* signals attributed belief; **When** introduces the move to America; **So** marks a consequence. Track **they / them / it** carefully: who carved, what vegetable was used before, and what replaced it? Distinguish legend from documented practice.

**Global retrieval (2 min):** Close the page. Rebuild **practice → possible origin → language/objects → changed tradition → modern conversation** in your own words. Reopen only the one sentence that broke your model. **Evidence limit:** the author presents possible roots and a folk story, not definitive historical proof of every detail.

## Speaking · 4 min

Answer aloud in order; 20–45 seconds each, no written script.

1. What do children do when they go trick-or-treating, according to the article?
2. How do **scary**, **afraid**, and **spooky** differ in the author's examples?
3. Why does the author say **Some people believe** instead of stating the Celtic origin as a certain fact?
4. How did a lantern-making custom change when people came to America?
5. Would a Halloween-style event work in your university? Use **go back to** to compare it with a tradition you know, and name one limitation.
